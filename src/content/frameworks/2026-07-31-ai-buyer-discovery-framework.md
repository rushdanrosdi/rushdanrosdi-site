## Framework purpose

The AI Buyer Discovery Framework is a structured method for examining how a brand appears, is understood and is considered when buyers use AI systems during commercial research.

It does not treat AI visibility as a single ranking position.

It treats buyer discovery as a sequence of questions, responses, sources, comparisons and decisions.

The framework is designed to help founders, fractional CMOs and B2B marketing teams answer a more useful question:

> When a potential buyer asks AI systems for help, where does our brand enter the decision — and where does it disappear?

## Core principle

Traditional search analysis often begins with keywords.

AI-assisted buyer research often begins with a situation.

The buyer may describe:

- a problem;
- an operational constraint;
- a desired outcome;
- a category they are exploring;
- a set of vendors they are comparing;
- a credibility concern;
- a risk they want to reduce.

That leads to the working principle behind this framework:

> Keywords tell us what the market is called. Buyer questions tell us how the market is decided.

## Framework overview

The framework contains nine connected stages:

1. Buyer situation
2. Buyer questions
3. Prompt set
4. AI response observation
5. Competitor comparison
6. Citation and source analysis
7. Entity understanding
8. Visibility gap
9. Priority actions

Each stage creates evidence for the next.

The objective is not to manufacture a perfect score.

The objective is to build a body of evidence that supports better commercial decisions.

---

## 1. Buyer situation

The framework begins with the buyer's real situation.

Before testing any AI system, define:

- who the buyer is;
- what type of organisation they represent;
- what problem they are trying to solve;
- what constraints influence the decision;
- what level of risk they are carrying;
- what stage of the buying journey they are in.

A generic prompt such as:

> Best cybersecurity software

contains very little commercial context.

A buyer-situation prompt such as:

> Which cybersecurity platforms are suitable for a 200-person financial-services company that needs strong audit evidence and cannot support a six-month implementation?

contains a real decision environment.

That context can change which brands appear and how they are described.

### Output

A concise buyer-situation definition for the category being studied.

---

## 2. Buyer questions

The second stage converts the buyer situation into realistic commercial questions.

The questions should reflect how a buyer moves through discovery.

Typical groups include:

- problem recognition;
- category discovery;
- vendor recommendation;
- comparison;
- fit assessment;
- credibility validation;
- risk reduction.

A useful question set does not rely on imagination alone.

It can be built from:

- sales-call notes;
- CRM records;
- proposal objections;
- support tickets;
- customer interviews;
- competitor comparisons;
- procurement questions;
- review sites;
- communities and forums;
- Google Search Console data;
- website-search data.

### Output

A structured set of buyer questions covering the decision journey.

---

## 3. Prompt set

One prompt is an observation.

A prompt set is the beginning of evidence.

The prompt set should test the same commercial category from multiple angles.

It should include variations in:

- buyer stage;
- company size;
- industry;
- geography;
- operational constraint;
- purchase criteria;
- competitor context;
- risk tolerance.

The goal is not to force a brand mention.

The goal is to observe whether the brand appears naturally across realistic buyer situations.

### Output

A repeatable prompt set that can be tested across selected AI platforms.

---

## 4. AI response observation

Each response should be recorded systematically.

The audit should capture more than whether the brand appears.

For every response, record:

- brand inclusion or omission;
- position within the answer;
- recommendation strength;
- wording used to describe the brand;
- accuracy of the description;
- competitors included;
- confidence or uncertainty;
- qualifications or warnings;
- differences between AI platforms;
- changes caused by buyer-context variations.

This stage turns screenshots into structured observations.

### Output

An evidence log of AI responses across the prompt set.

---

## 5. Competitor comparison

AI visibility is relative.

A brand can appear in an answer and still lose the commercial comparison.

The framework therefore examines:

- which competitors appear more frequently;
- which competitors are recommended more strongly;
- which attributes are associated with each brand;
- which use cases each competitor appears to own;
- where the brand is excluded;
- whether competitor positioning is clearer or more credible.

The objective is not merely to count mentions.

It is to understand the consideration set AI systems are constructing.

### Output

A competitor pattern showing where the brand gains or loses consideration.

---

## 6. Citation and source analysis

AI systems do not create commercial understanding from a company's website alone.

They may rely on or reflect information from:

- third-party articles;
- review platforms;
- directories;
- media coverage;
- analyst material;
- partner pages;
- community discussions;
- documentation;
- customer stories;
- structured website content.

This stage examines:

- which sources support the answer;
- whether those sources are current;
- whether they are authoritative;
- whether the brand is represented consistently;
- whether competitors have stronger third-party support;
- whether important claims are unsupported or absent.

Citation analysis helps distinguish a website problem from an authority problem.

### Output

A source map identifying which external and first-party signals influence the AI response pattern.

---

## 7. Entity understanding

A brand may be visible but misunderstood.

This stage evaluates whether AI systems understand:

- what the company does;
- which category it belongs to;
- who it serves;
- where it operates;
- how it differs from competitors;
- which products or services it offers;
- which claims are current;
- whether people, products and organisations are connected correctly.

Common risks include:

- outdated descriptions;
- category confusion;
- mixed brand names;
- inaccurate geography;
- unclear service boundaries;
- inconsistent founder or company information;
- legacy positioning dominating current positioning.

### Output

An entity-understanding assessment covering accuracy, consistency and category clarity.

---

## 8. Visibility gap

The visibility gap is the difference between:

- how the business wants to be understood;
- how buyers need to discover it;
- how AI systems currently represent it.

The gap should be interpreted by buyer stage.

Examples:

- absent during problem recognition;
- visible during category discovery but absent during recommendation;
- included in recommendations but weak in comparison;
- mentioned accurately but unsupported by citations;
- repeatedly confused with another category;
- strong in one AI platform and absent in another.

This stage converts observations into commercial meaning.

### Output

A prioritised list of buyer-discovery risks and missed opportunities.

---

## 9. Priority actions

The framework ends with action.

Actions should be prioritised according to the observed gap, not selected from a generic GEO checklist.

Possible action areas include:

- website positioning;
- category language;
- service and product pages;
- educational content;
- comparison content;
- case studies;
- structured data;
- entity consistency;
- founder and author authority;
- third-party citations;
- digital PR;
- partner pages;
- review coverage;
- documentation;
- technical accessibility;
- ongoing monitoring.

Each action should connect to a specific finding.

### Output

A 30-, 60- or 90-day action plan tied directly to the evidence.

---

## Evidence recorded

A practical implementation of the framework may record:

- buyer-question category;
- exact prompt;
- AI platform;
- date and time;
- brand mention;
- mention context;
- recommendation strength;
- competitors;
- source or citation;
- description accuracy;
- message consistency;
- buyer-stage relevance;
- observed risk;
- recommended action.

This creates a repeatable research record rather than a collection of isolated screenshots.

## What the framework does not claim

The AI Buyer Discovery Framework does not claim:

- permanent AI rankings;
- perfect reproducibility;
- guaranteed inclusion;
- direct revenue attribution from every AI mention;
- control over model outputs;
- that one audit represents all future behaviour.

AI responses can change because of model updates, source changes, location, account context, prompt wording, browsing access, recency and platform behaviour.

The framework is designed to identify patterns, not pretend that AI answers are fixed.

## Commercial outputs

A completed analysis can produce:

- an executive summary;
- a buyer-question map;
- a multi-platform evidence appendix;
- a competitor comparison;
- a citation and source map;
- an entity-risk assessment;
- a prioritised 90-day action plan;
- a partner briefing;
- an optional client presentation.

The commercial value is not the prompt itself.

The value is the interpretation:

- what the pattern means;
- why the pattern exists;
- where the risk is;
- what should change first.
