## Framework purpose

The AI Buyer Discovery Framework is a structured method for examining how a brand appears, is understood and is considered when buyers use AI systems during commercial research.

It does not treat AI visibility as a single ranking position.

It treats buyer discovery as a sequence of buyer situations, questions, response patterns, comparisons, sources, entity interpretations and commercial decisions.

The framework is designed to help founders, fractional CMOs and B2B marketing teams answer a more useful question:

> When a potential buyer asks AI systems for help, where does our brand enter the decision — where does it weaken, and where does it disappear?

## Core principle

Traditional search analysis often begins with keywords.

AI-assisted buyer research often begins with a situation.

The buyer may describe:

- a problem;
- an operational constraint;
- a desired outcome;
- a category they are exploring;
- a set of vendors they are comparing;
- a credibility concern;
- a risk they want to reduce.

That leads to the working principle behind this framework:

> Keywords tell us what the market is called. Buyer questions tell us how the market is decided.

## Framework overview

The framework contains nine connected stages:

1. Buyer situation
2. Buyer questions
3. Prompt set
4. AI response pattern interpretation
5. Competitor comparison
6. Citation and source analysis
7. Entity understanding
8. Visibility gap
9. Priority actions

Each stage creates evidence for the next.

The objective is not to manufacture a perfect score.

The objective is to build a body of evidence that supports better commercial decisions.

---

## 1. Buyer situation

The framework begins with the buyer's real situation.

Before testing any AI system, define:

- who the buyer is;
- what type of organisation they represent;
- what problem they are trying to solve;
- what constraints influence the decision;
- what level of risk they are carrying;
- what stage of the buying journey they are in.

A generic prompt such as:

> Best cybersecurity software

contains very little commercial context.

A buyer-situation prompt such as:

> Which cybersecurity platforms are suitable for a 200-person financial-services company that needs strong audit evidence and cannot support a six-month implementation?

contains a real decision environment.

That context can change which brands appear, which competitors are introduced and how recommendations are framed.

### Output

A concise buyer-situation definition for the category being studied.

---

## 2. Buyer questions

The second stage converts the buyer situation into realistic commercial questions.

The questions should reflect how a buyer moves through discovery.

Typical groups include:

- problem recognition;
- category discovery;
- vendor recommendation;
- comparison;
- fit assessment;
- credibility validation;
- risk reduction.

A useful question set does not rely on imagination alone.

It can be built from:

- sales-call notes;
- CRM records;
- proposal objections;
- support tickets;
- customer interviews;
- competitor comparisons;
- procurement questions;
- review sites;
- communities and forums;
- Google Search Console data;
- website-search data;
- public comments and discussion threads;
- recurring questions from direct conversations.

Conversation can therefore become a source of buyer-question intelligence when the same concerns, terminology or objections appear repeatedly.

### Output

A structured set of buyer questions covering the decision journey and grounded, where possible, in real buyer language.

---

## 3. Prompt set

One prompt is an observation.

A prompt set is the beginning of evidence.

The prompt set should test the same commercial category from multiple angles.

It should include controlled variations in:

- buyer stage;
- company size;
- industry;
- geography;
- operational constraint;
- purchase criteria;
- competitor context;
- risk tolerance.

The goal is not to force a brand mention.

The goal is to observe whether the brand appears naturally across realistic buyer situations and whether small changes in context materially change the answer.

### Output

A repeatable prompt set with controlled variations that can be tested across selected AI platforms.

---

## 4. AI response pattern interpretation

A single AI answer is an observation.

The framework is interested in the pattern created across multiple observations.

For every response, record:

- brand inclusion or omission;
- position within the answer where relevant;
- recommendation strength;
- wording used to describe the brand;
- accuracy of the description;
- competitors included;
- confidence or uncertainty;
- qualifications or warnings;
- differences between AI platforms;
- changes caused by buyer-context variations.

Then compare responses across the prompt set.

Look for repeated patterns such as:

- frequent inclusion;
- repeated omission;
- strong or weak recommendation wording;
- buyer-stage coverage;
- competitor co-occurrence;
- description consistency;
- platform variation;
- sensitivity to buyer context.

The objective is not to treat one screenshot as a ranking result.

The objective is to determine whether a repeatable visibility pattern is emerging.

### Output

An evidence log plus a response-pattern summary showing how the brand behaves across prompts, buyer situations and selected platforms.

---

## 5. Competitor comparison

AI visibility is relative.

A brand can appear in an answer and still lose the commercial comparison.

The framework therefore examines:

- which competitors appear more frequently;
- which competitors are recommended more strongly;
- which attributes are associated with each brand;
- which use cases or buyer situations each competitor appears to own;
- where the brand is excluded;
- whether competitor positioning is clearer or more credible;
- whether competitors are supported by stronger or fresher evidence.

The competitive set should not be assumed in advance.

AI systems may introduce adjacent providers, substitute solutions or alternative buying models that the company does not normally track.

The objective is not merely to count mentions.

It is to understand the consideration set being constructed around the buyer's question.

### Output

A competitor pattern showing where the brand gains or loses consideration, which attributes competitors appear to own and where the competitive set differs from internal assumptions.

---

## 6. Citation and source analysis

AI systems do not create commercial understanding from a company's website alone.

They may rely on or reflect information from:

- first-party website pages;
- third-party articles;
- review platforms;
- directories;
- media coverage;
- analyst material;
- partner pages;
- community discussions;
- documentation;
- customer stories;
- profiles;
- structured website content;
- older or archived material.

This stage examines:

- which sources support or appear to shape the answer;
- whether those sources are current;
- whether they are authoritative and relevant;
- whether important claims are supported;
- whether competitors have stronger third-party support;
- whether old sources reinforce outdated positioning;
- whether different sources describe the brand consistently.

Source analysis should distinguish between volume and usefulness.

A large number of weak or conflicting sources does not automatically create strong authority.

### Source-to-entity relationship

The output of source analysis feeds directly into entity understanding.

The key question is not only:

> Which sources exist?

It is also:

> What combined meaning do those sources create about the brand?

Repeated source signals can reinforce category, audience, geography, expertise and evidence.

Conflicting source signals can create competing interpretations.

### Output

A source map showing the first-party and external signals that appear to influence the response pattern, including freshness, consistency, authority and possible conflicts.

---

## 7. Entity understanding

A brand may be visible but misunderstood.

This stage evaluates whether AI systems appear to understand:

- what the company does;
- which category it belongs to;
- who it serves;
- where it operates;
- how it differs from competitors;
- which products or services it offers;
- which claims are current;
- which expertise is repeatedly associated with it;
- whether people, products and organisations are connected correctly.

The analysis should separate two views.

### Intended identity

What the business wants the market to understand:

- desired category;
- target buyer;
- geography;
- positioning;
- differentiation;
- expertise;
- proof.

### Interpreted identity

What AI responses and available sources appear to communicate:

- actual category association;
- perceived audience;
- inferred geography;
- associated competitors;
- visible evidence;
- recurring attributes;
- conflicting or outdated descriptions.

The difference between these two views is an entity-understanding gap.

### Consistency principle

Consistency does not require identical wording across every source.

It requires different sources to reinforce compatible meaning.

For example, a homepage, profile, case study and article may use different language while still reinforcing the same relationships between:

```text
Brand
→ Category
→ Audience
→ Geography
→ Expertise
→ Evidence
```

Common risks include:

- outdated descriptions;
- category confusion;
- mixed brand names;
- inaccurate geography;
- unclear service boundaries;
- inconsistent founder or company information;
- legacy positioning dominating current positioning;
- too many valid but unprioritised titles or categories.

### Output

An entity-understanding assessment comparing intended identity with interpreted identity across category, audience, geography, differentiation, evidence and consistency.

---

## 8. Visibility gap

The visibility gap is the difference between:

- how the business wants to be understood;
- how buyers need to discover it;
- how AI systems currently represent and consider it.

The gap should be interpreted by buyer stage and commercial consequence.

Examples:

- absent during problem recognition;
- visible during category discovery but absent during recommendation;
- included in recommendations but weak in comparison;
- frequently mentioned but rarely recommended;
- mentioned accurately but unsupported by strong evidence;
- repeatedly confused with another category;
- represented through outdated positioning;
- strong in one AI platform and absent in another;
- recognised as an entity but not associated with the buyer situation the company wants to own.

A visibility gap is therefore not always simple absence.

It can also be a gap in interpretation, evidence, recommendation strength, buyer-stage coverage or competitive positioning.

### Output

A prioritised list of buyer-discovery risks and missed opportunities, with each gap linked to the stage where the problem appears.

---

## 9. Priority actions

The framework ends with action.

Actions should be prioritised according to the observed gap, buyer impact and likely cause — not selected from a generic GEO checklist.

A useful action hierarchy is:

### 1. Correct

Fix information that is inaccurate, conflicting, obsolete or technically broken.

Examples:

- wrong category;
- outdated service;
- incorrect geography;
- obsolete profile;
- conflicting company description;
- inaccessible or broken source.

### 2. Clarify

Make the intended identity easier to interpret.

Examples:

- establish the primary category;
- define the target buyer;
- explain the main use case;
- clarify product versus service;
- align founder and company positioning;
- make geography and market coverage explicit.

### 3. Prove

Add evidence that supports recommendation confidence.

Examples:

- case studies;
- client outcomes;
- technical documentation;
- comparison evidence;
- credible third-party references;
- partner validation;
- independent reviews.

### 4. Expand

Increase coverage only after the core identity and evidence are sufficiently clear.

Examples:

- additional educational content;
- buyer-stage content;
- supporting Lab Notes;
- relevant third-party distribution;
- expanded comparison coverage;
- wider authority-building activity.

This sequence matters.

Expanding before correcting and clarifying can distribute confusion more efficiently.

### Prioritisation criteria

Each action should be evaluated against:

- buyer impact;
- strategic fit;
- confidence in the diagnosis;
- implementation effort;
- dependency on third parties;
- time to evidence;
- measurement difficulty.

Each action should connect to a specific finding.

### Output

A ranked 30-, 60- or 90-day action plan that explains what to correct, clarify, prove or expand, and which evidence should be re-tested.

---

## Feedback loop

Priority actions are not the end of the research cycle.

After implementation, the relevant buyer questions should be tested again.

The feedback loop is:

```text
Observe
→ Diagnose
→ Act
→ Re-test
→ Compare
→ Refine
```

Useful new evidence may also come from distribution and conversation around the work.

For example:

- a buyer asks a question not present in the original prompt set;
- a public discussion reveals confusing terminology;
- a client exposes a missing decision criterion;
- a competitor comparison introduces a previously ignored alternative;
- a source update changes how the brand is described.

These inputs may refine the next audit cycle without turning distribution or conversation into additional stages of the AI Buyer Discovery Framework.

The framework remains focused on buyer discovery.

Publishing, distribution, conversation and longitudinal measurement sit in the broader Authority Systems layer around it.

## Evidence recorded

A practical implementation of the framework may record:

- buyer-situation definition;
- buyer-question category;
- exact prompt;
- prompt variation;
- AI platform;
- date and time;
- brand mention;
- mention context;
- recommendation strength;
- competitors;
- source or citation;
- source freshness;
- source type;
- description accuracy;
- entity consistency;
- buyer-stage relevance;
- intended identity;
- interpreted identity;
- observed gap;
- likely cause;
- recommended action;
- priority level;
- re-test date.

This creates a repeatable research record rather than a collection of isolated screenshots.

## What the framework does not claim

The AI Buyer Discovery Framework does not claim:

- permanent AI rankings;
- perfect reproducibility;
- guaranteed inclusion;
- direct revenue attribution from every AI mention;
- control over model outputs;
- that one audit represents all future behaviour;
- that every visible source is necessarily used by every AI system;
- that correlation between a published change and an AI response proves causation.

AI responses can change because of model updates, source changes, location, account context, prompt wording, browsing access, recency and platform behaviour.

The framework is designed to identify patterns, interpret gaps and guide action without pretending that AI answers are fixed.

## Commercial outputs

A completed analysis can produce:

- an executive summary;
- a buyer-situation definition;
- a buyer-question map;
- a repeatable prompt set;
- a multi-platform response-pattern appendix;
- a competitor consideration-set comparison;
- a citation and source map;
- an intended-versus-interpreted entity assessment;
- a prioritised visibility-gap register;
- a Correct / Clarify / Prove / Expand action plan;
- a re-test schedule;
- a partner briefing;
- an optional client presentation.

The commercial value is not the prompt itself.

The value is the interpretation:

- what the pattern means;
- why the pattern may exist;
- where the buyer-discovery risk is;
- what should change first;
- what evidence should be created;
- what should be re-tested next.

## Version history

### Version 1.1 — 10 August 2026

Refined the initial nine-stage methodology using findings developed through Lab Notes #001–#013.

Key changes:

- changed Stage 4 from single-response observation to response-pattern interpretation;
- made competitor consideration-set analysis more explicit;
- connected source analysis directly to entity understanding;
- added intended identity versus interpreted identity;
- clarified semantic consistency across entity signals;
- expanded visibility gaps beyond simple inclusion or omission;
- introduced the **Correct → Clarify → Prove → Expand** action hierarchy;
- added a formal re-test and feedback loop;
- reorganised supporting research by methodology area;
- clarified the boundary between the AI Buyer Discovery Framework and the broader Authority Systems layer.

### Version 1.0 — 31 July 2026

Initial public version.

Defined the original nine-stage AI Buyer Discovery Framework and connected it to the first three supporting Lab Notes.

## Working principle

> AI visibility is not a single answer to a single prompt. It is the pattern of how a brand is discovered, interpreted, compared, supported and considered across the buyer's decision journey.
