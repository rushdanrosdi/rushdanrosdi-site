A source list can tell us what exists.

A homepage. Three service pages. Two case studies. Five reviews. A partner page. Several LinkedIn posts. A few third-party mentions.

That is useful.

But it still leaves a more important question unanswered:

> Which buyer questions can this evidence actually support?

That is why I am starting to think a source inventory is not enough.

What we need is an evidence coverage map.

## Source presence does not guarantee buyer coverage

A company may have plenty of public evidence.

But that evidence may cluster around only one part of the buying decision.

For example, a brand might have strong material explaining what the company does, who founded it, how long it has operated and which services it offers.

But very little answering whether it has solved this exact problem before, whether it works in the buyer's environment, how it compares with alternatives, what evidence supports the claim or what risks the buyer should understand.

The source count looks healthy.

The buyer coverage is still weak.

## Buyer questions are a better unit of analysis

Instead of asking only:

> What sources do we have?

we can ask:

> What evidence supports each important buyer question?

Suppose the buyer-question set includes:

```text
What does this company do?
Who is it for?
Has it solved this problem before?
Why should I trust it?
How is it different?
Is it suitable for my situation?
What evidence supports the claim?
```

Now every proof asset can be mapped against the questions it genuinely supports.

The gaps become easier to see.

## One source can cover several questions

A detailed case study may support capability, buyer fit, implementation experience, credibility and outcome evidence.

A technical article may support expertise, mechanism and category understanding.

A customer review may support experience, trust and service quality.

A partner page may support external validation, ecosystem credibility and market relevance.

The same source can therefore contribute to several buyer questions.

This is useful because evidence should compound.

## But some questions may still have no proof

Imagine the coverage looks like this:

```text
Buyer Question                         Evidence Coverage

What does the company do?              Strong
Who is it for?                         Strong
Has it solved this before?             Supporting
Why should I trust it?                 Strong
How is it different?                   Weak
Is it suitable for my situation?       Weak
What evidence supports the claim?      Supporting
```

A simple source count would not show this pattern.

The evidence coverage map does.

## Coverage and strength are different dimensions

A buyer question may have many sources but still weak evidence.

Another question may have only one source but very strong evidence.

So the map should capture two things:

### Coverage

Does relevant evidence exist for this buyer question?

### Strength

How useful is that evidence?

A simple working model could use:

```text
None
Weak
Supporting
Strong
```

This is not meant to simulate a hidden AI ranking formula.

It is an analytical tool for deciding what the brand can credibly support today.

## Coverage should follow the buyer journey

Different questions matter at different stages.

### Problem recognition

- Does this problem exist?
- What causes it?
- What happens if it is ignored?

### Category discovery

- What kind of solution is suitable?
- What categories should I consider?

### Provider discovery

- Which companies can help?
- Which providers are relevant?

### Comparison

- How do these options differ?
- Which one fits my context?

### Validation

- Has this company done this before?
- What proof exists?
- Can I trust the claim?

A company may be strong at provider discovery but weak at validation.

Or highly credible at validation but almost invisible during category discovery.

That is a different kind of visibility gap.

## Evidence coverage can expose content waste

Without a coverage map, teams often publish where they already have plenty of support.

The company may continue producing more broad educational articles, more founder posts and more generic service explanations while the real evidence gap is:

> no visible proof that the company has solved the buyer's exact problem.

Publishing more in the already-strong area increases volume.

It does not close the important gap.

## Coverage improves prioritisation

For each high-value buyer question, ask:

1. Does relevant evidence exist?
2. How strong is it?
3. Is it first-party, third-party or both?
4. Is it current?
5. Is it easy to find?
6. Does it reinforce the intended entity?
7. Does it need to be created, clarified or redistributed?

Now the action plan can be tied directly to buyer uncertainty.

## The best next asset may already exist

Sometimes the coverage gap does not require creating new evidence.

The proof may already exist but be buried in an old case study, described with weak wording, isolated on one page, missing from the relevant service page, disconnected from current positioning or never distributed around the buyer question.

In those cases, the better action may be reuse and reframing rather than new production.

That connects evidence coverage back to leverage.

## Evidence coverage also helps with re-testing

If an intervention is meant to strengthen one buyer question, the re-test becomes more specific.

For example:

```text
Gap:
Weak proof for remote-site deployment experience

Action:
Publish and distribute a documented field deployment case study

Re-test:
Buyer questions involving remote deployment, resilience and implementation experience
```

The map creates a link between:

```text
Buyer Question
→ Evidence Gap
→ Action
→ Re-test
```

That is a useful research loop.

## A practical evidence coverage table

A simple implementation might include:

```text
Buyer Question
Buyer Stage
Existing Evidence
Evidence Strength
Source Type
Freshness
Current Gap
Priority Action
Re-test Prompt
```

This does not need to become complicated software on day one.

A spreadsheet is enough.

The value is in the structure.

## The commercial implication

A client does not need to know only how many sources mention the brand.

They need to know whether the public evidence actually helps a buyer make the decision.

That means the audit should show where evidence is strong, where it is weak, which buyer questions are unsupported, which proof can be reused and which new proof deserves priority.

This is much closer to commercial reality than a source-count report.

## The working principle

My current working principle is:

> A source inventory tells us what exists. An evidence coverage map tells us whether the right evidence exists for the buyer questions that matter.

That is the difference between collecting sources and building an evidence system.
