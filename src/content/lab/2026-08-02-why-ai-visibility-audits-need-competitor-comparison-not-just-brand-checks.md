A basic AI visibility check usually asks:

> Does our brand appear?

That is a useful starting point.

It is not enough for a serious audit.

A brand can appear in an AI answer and still lose the buyer's attention to a competitor that is described more clearly, recommended more strongly, supported by better sources or associated with a more relevant use case.

The real question is not only whether the brand appears.

It is:

> How does the brand compare with the alternatives that AI systems place beside it?

## AI-assisted discovery creates a consideration set

When a buyer asks for recommendations, comparisons or suitable vendors, AI systems often construct a shortlist.

That shortlist may include:

- established market leaders;
- niche specialists;
- local providers;
- lower-cost alternatives;
- enterprise options;
- technically strong challengers;
- brands with strong third-party validation.

The shortlist is the AI-generated consideration set.

A brand may be visible inside that set, but visibility alone does not tell us:

- whether it is recommended first;
- whether it is described accurately;
- whether it is positioned as a primary or secondary option;
- whether it is associated with the right buyer situation;
- whether competitors receive stronger evidence and framing.

That is why competitor comparison is necessary.

## Brand checks can create false confidence

Suppose an audit tests ten buyer questions.

The brand appears in six.

At first glance, that looks positive.

But a deeper comparison may reveal:

- Competitor A appears in nine;
- Competitor B is recommended first in seven;
- Competitor C owns the enterprise use case;
- the audited brand is usually framed as a possible alternative;
- the audited brand receives weaker source support;
- the audited brand disappears when the buyer asks for proof, fit or implementation confidence.

The brand is visible.

But it may not be competitive.

A brand-only audit would miss that.

## What should be compared

A useful competitor comparison should examine more than mention counts.

### 1. Frequency of appearance

Record how often each brand appears across the buyer-question set.

This helps identify:

- dominant brands;
- recurring alternatives;
- niche players;
- brands that only appear under specific conditions.

Frequency is not the final answer, but it reveals the structure of the consideration set.

### 2. Order of appearance

In some answers, order matters.

A brand listed first may receive more attention than one added at the end.

But order should be interpreted carefully.

The first brand is not always the strongest recommendation.

A later brand may be framed as the best fit for the buyer's exact situation.

So the audit should record both:

- order of appearance;
- recommendation wording.

### 3. Recommendation strength

Compare whether each brand is framed as:

- the best option;
- a strong choice;
- a suitable option;
- a niche alternative;
- a budget option;
- an enterprise option;
- a possible fit;
- a risky choice;
- a poor fit.

This is more commercially meaningful than simple inclusion.

### 4. Attribute ownership

AI systems often associate brands with specific attributes.

Examples include:

- ease of use;
- technical depth;
- enterprise readiness;
- lower price;
- implementation support;
- local expertise;
- integration capability;
- speed;
- reliability;
- compliance;
- customer service.

The audit should identify which competitor appears to own each attribute.

If one competitor is repeatedly associated with reliability and another with ease of implementation, that pattern matters.

It shows how AI systems differentiate the market.

### 5. Buyer-situation ownership

Competitors may appear under different buyer conditions.

One brand may dominate when the buyer asks for:

- enterprise scale;
- small-team simplicity;
- local support;
- regulated environments;
- low budget;
- fast deployment;
- technical customisation;
- managed service.

This creates buyer-situation ownership.

A competitor may not appear everywhere, but it may strongly own the exact situation that matters most.

### 6. Evidence strength

Compare the evidence supporting each brand.

Useful signals include:

- first-party documentation;
- case studies;
- independent reviews;
- industry articles;
- comparison pages;
- directories;
- analyst mentions;
- credible third-party references;
- current product information.

A competitor with stronger source support may receive more confident and detailed recommendations.

### 7. Description accuracy

A competitor comparison should also record whether brands are described correctly.

A brand can lose because AI systems misunderstand:

- what it offers;
- who it serves;
- where it operates;
- what makes it different;
- whether it is a product, service or consultancy;
- whether it is active and current.

An inaccurate description is a visibility problem even when the brand appears.

## Competitor comparison reveals the real gap

A visibility gap is not always simple absence.

It can be:

- lower frequency;
- weaker recommendation;
- poorer attribute association;
- weaker evidence;
- inaccurate positioning;
- limited buyer-stage coverage;
- absence from important buyer situations;
- stronger competitor framing.

These are different problems.

They require different actions.

For example:

### Gap: low frequency

Possible response:

- strengthen category relevance;
- improve topic coverage;
- expand credible mentions;
- clarify the brand's association with the buyer problem.

### Gap: weak recommendation

Possible response:

- improve differentiation;
- publish stronger proof;
- clarify fit;
- support claims with evidence.

### Gap: competitor owns a key attribute

Possible response:

- create stronger first-party evidence;
- earn third-party validation;
- publish comparison-relevant content;
- make the brand's distinctive strength easier to verify.

### Gap: inaccurate description

Possible response:

- correct inconsistent entity information;
- update outdated pages;
- align profiles and descriptions;
- improve structured brand signals.

The comparison determines the action.

## The danger of checking only direct competitors

A useful audit should not assume the competitive set in advance.

AI systems may introduce alternatives that the company does not normally track.

These may include:

- substitute products;
- adjacent service providers;
- do-it-yourself options;
- larger platforms;
- specialist firms;
- local agencies;
- internal team solutions;
- different buying models.

This matters because the buyer may not define the category the same way the company does.

The AI-generated consideration set may be broader than the internal competitor list.

That is commercially useful evidence.

## A practical comparison record

For each buyer question, record:

- exact prompt;
- platform;
- date;
- brands included;
- order of appearance;
- recommendation strength;
- key attributes assigned to each brand;
- buyer situation attached to each brand;
- source or citation support;
- description accuracy;
- warnings or conditions;
- likely commercial implication.

Over multiple questions, patterns begin to emerge.

## The output should not be a vanity score

A single score such as:

> AI visibility score: 72/100

may be easy to present.

But it can hide the real issue.

A better audit output explains:

- who the brand is competing with;
- where each competitor is stronger;
- which buyer situations the brand wins or loses;
- which attributes competitors own;
- where evidence is weak;
- what actions should be prioritised.

The purpose is not to produce a flattering number.

The purpose is to improve the brand's position inside AI-assisted buyer discovery.

## The working principle

My current working principle is:

> A brand's AI visibility has no commercial meaning until it is compared with the alternatives AI systems recommend to the same buyer.

That is why competitor comparison is not an optional layer.

It is part of the audit itself.
