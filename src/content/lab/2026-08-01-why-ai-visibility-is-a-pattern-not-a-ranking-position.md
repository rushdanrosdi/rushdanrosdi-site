Traditional search analysis often asks:

> Where do we rank?

That question works because search results are usually presented as an ordered list.

AI-assisted discovery is different.

A buyer may ask ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or another answer engine for recommendations, comparisons, explanations or risk checks. The response may contain several brands, no brands, different wording, different sources and different levels of confidence.

There may be no stable first position.

There may not even be a visible list.

That means AI visibility should not be interpreted as a single ranking position.

It should be interpreted as a **pattern**.

## Why ranking language can be misleading

Ranking language suggests three things:

1. there is one ordered result;
2. that order is stable;
3. position is the main measure of value.

AI answers can violate all three.

A brand may:

- appear in one answer and disappear in another;
- be mentioned without being recommended;
- be recommended for one buyer situation but rejected for another;
- appear strongly in one platform and weakly in another;
- be described accurately but supported by weak sources;
- be listed after a competitor but framed as the better fit;
- dominate category explanations but vanish during vendor comparison.

A single position cannot capture that.

## Inclusion is not the same as recommendation

One of the first distinctions an audit should make is between:

- omission;
- mention;
- inclusion in a shortlist;
- positive recommendation;
- conditional recommendation;
- strong recommendation;
- warning or negative framing.

These outcomes are commercially different.

For example:

> Company A provides enterprise workflow software.

is a mention.

> Company A is a strong option for regulated enterprises that need advanced audit controls.

is a recommendation.

> Company A may be suitable, but implementation is complex and pricing is less transparent.

is a conditional recommendation.

Counting all three as the same visibility event would hide the actual buyer impact.

## Pattern #1: Frequency of inclusion

The first useful pattern is how often the brand appears across realistic buyer questions.

This should be observed across:

- problem questions;
- category questions;
- recommendation questions;
- comparison questions;
- fit questions;
- credibility questions.

A brand that appears once in fifteen questions has a different visibility profile from one that appears in ten.

But frequency alone is not enough.

A brand can appear often and still be described poorly.

## Pattern #2: Buyer-stage coverage

A brand may be visible at one stage of the decision journey but absent at another.

For example:

- present during category discovery;
- absent during vendor recommendations;
- included during comparison;
- weak during credibility validation.

This creates a buyer-stage gap.

The brand may be known but not trusted.

Or understood but not shortlisted.

Or shortlisted but not differentiated.

A useful audit should identify where the brand enters and exits the buyer journey.

## Pattern #3: Recommendation strength

The wording used matters.

AI systems may frame a brand as:

- a market leader;
- a niche option;
- a budget choice;
- an enterprise choice;
- a technically strong option;
- a risky option;
- a possible alternative;
- a poor fit for a specific situation.

Recommendation strength should therefore be recorded separately from simple inclusion.

The commercial question is not only:

> Did the brand appear?

It is:

> How strongly did the answer support the brand as a relevant choice?

## Pattern #4: Competitor co-occurrence

AI visibility is relative.

A brand can appear and still lose the comparison.

Useful observations include:

- which competitors appear alongside the brand;
- which competitors appear more frequently;
- which competitors are recommended first;
- which attributes are associated with each competitor;
- which buyer situations each competitor appears to own;
- whether the brand is consistently framed as a secondary option.

This reveals the consideration set AI systems are constructing.

## Pattern #5: Description consistency

A brand may be visible but represented inconsistently.

Across answers, AI systems may describe the same company as:

- a software platform;
- a consultancy;
- a managed service;
- a hardware provider;
- a local business;
- a global company.

If those descriptions conflict, visibility becomes less useful.

A strong pattern should show consistency in:

- category;
- audience;
- geography;
- product or service;
- differentiation;
- positioning;
- evidence.

Inconsistent descriptions often indicate weak or fragmented entity signals.

## Pattern #6: Source and citation support

Some answers are supported by strong sources.

Others are based on vague, outdated or secondary material.

The audit should record:

- whether sources are cited;
- whether the sources are first-party or third-party;
- whether the information is current;
- whether competitors have stronger source support;
- whether important claims are repeated across credible sources;
- whether outdated pages are shaping the answer.

A brand may appear often but remain weakly supported.

That creates a credibility risk.

## Pattern #7: Platform variation

Different AI systems may produce different answers because they use different models, sources, browsing methods and response styles.

A brand may be:

- visible in Perplexity;
- absent in ChatGPT;
- described differently in Gemini;
- supported by different citations across platforms.

This does not automatically mean one platform is correct and another is wrong.

It means the visibility pattern is platform-dependent.

A useful audit should compare platforms without pretending that they are identical.

## Pattern #8: Sensitivity to buyer context

Small changes in buyer context can change the answer.

Compare:

> Best CRM for a growing business

with:

> Best CRM for a 40-person B2B SaaS company that needs strong HubSpot integration and a small operations team.

The second question may produce a completely different shortlist.

The audit should therefore test changes in:

- company size;
- industry;
- geography;
- budget;
- technical requirement;
- implementation capacity;
- compliance need;
- urgency;
- preferred buying model.

If a brand appears only under very narrow conditions, that is an important finding.

## Why screenshots are weak evidence

A screenshot can reveal a useful clue.

It cannot, by itself, show a stable visibility pattern.

One screenshot does not tell us:

- whether the answer repeats;
- whether the result changes across prompts;
- whether competitors appear more often;
- whether the brand is recommended or merely mentioned;
- whether the source pattern is reliable;
- whether the answer changes across platforms;
- whether the brand survives comparison and validation.

Screenshots are useful as evidence records.

They are not the methodology.

## What should be recorded

For each buyer question, a practical evidence log may include:

- exact prompt;
- buyer-stage category;
- AI platform;
- date and time;
- brand included or omitted;
- recommendation strength;
- competitor names;
- order of appearance where relevant;
- brand description;
- description accuracy;
- cited or reflected sources;
- confidence or uncertainty;
- warnings or conditions;
- commercial interpretation;
- recommended action.

This makes patterns visible.

## What a pattern can reveal

Different patterns point to different problems.

### Frequent omission

Possible causes:

- weak category association;
- limited authority;
- poor third-party coverage;
- unclear positioning;
- low source visibility.

### Frequent mention but weak recommendation

Possible causes:

- weak differentiation;
- unclear evidence;
- stronger competitor framing;
- limited fit for the tested buyer situation.

### Strong visibility but inaccurate description

Possible causes:

- outdated sources;
- inconsistent entity information;
- legacy positioning;
- mixed brand signals.

### Strong in one platform, weak in another

Possible causes:

- different source coverage;
- platform-specific retrieval behaviour;
- uneven citation footprint;
- recency differences.

### Strong early, weak late

Possible causes:

- good educational visibility;
- poor comparison evidence;
- weak credibility validation;
- limited proof of fit.

The pattern guides the intervention.

## The commercial implication

A brand does not need a permanent number-one position.

It needs to be:

- included often enough;
- described accurately;
- recommended in relevant situations;
- supported by credible evidence;
- competitive during comparison;
- visible at the right stages of the buying journey.

That is a more useful commercial objective than asking:

> Are we ranking first in AI?

## The working principle

My current working principle is:

> AI visibility is not a position. It is the repeated pattern of inclusion, interpretation, recommendation and support across buyer questions.

That is what should be measured.
