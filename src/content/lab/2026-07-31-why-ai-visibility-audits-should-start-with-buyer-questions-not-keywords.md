Traditional search audits usually begin with keywords.

That makes sense when the primary task is to understand how people search, how often a term is used and which pages compete for rankings.

AI-assisted discovery changes the starting point.

A buyer using ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity or another answer engine is often not entering a compact keyword. The buyer is describing a situation, asking for alternatives, comparing trade-offs, testing fit and looking for reassurance before making contact.

That means an AI visibility audit should not begin with a keyword list.

It should begin with **buyer questions**.

## Keywords describe a topic. Questions reveal a decision.

A keyword such as:

> project management software

describes a category.

A buyer question such as:

> Which project management platforms are suitable for a 50-person engineering team that needs strong permission controls and does not want an enterprise implementation?

reveals much more:

- company size;
- operational context;
- technical requirements;
- buying constraints;
- implementation preference;
- comparison criteria;
- likely stage of the decision.

The keyword identifies the market.

The question reveals the buying situation.

AI systems are often asked to interpret that full situation, not merely match a phrase to a page.

## Buyer discovery is a sequence

A real buyer rarely asks one question and stops.

The journey often moves through several stages:

1. Understand the problem.
2. Identify the category.
3. Discover possible vendors.
4. Compare options.
5. Test suitability.
6. Validate credibility.
7. Reduce perceived risk.

Each stage creates a different type of question.

For example:

### Problem recognition

> Why does our remote site video feed keep failing even when the router shows a mobile signal?

### Category discovery

> What type of connectivity setup is used for reliable video streaming from remote industrial sites?

### Vendor discovery

> Which companies provide multi-carrier connectivity for remote monitoring projects?

### Comparison

> What is the difference between a standard dual-SIM router and true multi-link bonding?

### Fit assessment

> Is multi-SIM bonding suitable for flood monitoring cameras in areas with unstable mobile coverage?

### Credibility validation

> Which providers have experience with low-latency streaming and mission-critical field connectivity?

These questions belong to the same commercial journey, but they are not interchangeable.

A brand may appear in one stage and disappear in another.

That pattern matters more than a single answer.

## Why keyword-first audits can miss the real risk

A conventional keyword list can still be useful. It helps define category language, demand themes and known search behaviour.

But it can create three blind spots when used as the starting point for AI visibility.

### 1. It compresses the buyer’s context

Keywords remove the details that influence recommendation.

Two buyers may use the same category term but have completely different requirements, budgets and risk tolerance.

AI systems often respond differently once those constraints are included.

### 2. It overweights known terminology

Buyers do not always know the correct category name.

They may describe symptoms, operational problems or desired outcomes instead.

A company can be well positioned for a recognised keyword and still be absent when the buyer explains the problem in natural language.

### 3. It encourages ranking-style thinking

Keyword audits often lead to questions such as:

- Where do we rank?
- Which page appears?
- What is our position?

AI answers are less stable and less linear.

The more useful questions are:

- Are we included?
- In which buyer situations?
- How are we described?
- Which competitors appear instead?
- What evidence supports the answer?
- Is our positioning represented accurately?

That requires a different audit design.

## Build the question set from buyer reality

A strong buyer-question set should not be invented from imagination alone.

It can be built from evidence already available inside the business:

- sales call notes;
- discovery-call recordings;
- proposal objections;
- support tickets;
- CRM notes;
- customer interviews;
- competitor comparisons;
- product demos;
- website search data;
- Google Search Console queries;
- forum discussions;
- industry communities;
- reviews;
- questions asked during procurement.

These sources reveal how buyers actually describe the problem.

The audit should preserve that language where possible.

The goal is not to produce the most elegant prompt.

The goal is to simulate the questions that could influence a real commercial decision.

## A practical question architecture

For one commercial category, I would usually begin with several question groups.

### 1. Problem questions

These test whether the brand is connected to the underlying problem.

Example:

> How can a company maintain a stable live camera feed from a remote site with inconsistent mobile coverage?

### 2. Category questions

These test whether the brand is associated with the relevant solution category.

Example:

> What technologies are used to improve connectivity resilience at remote monitoring sites?

### 3. Recommendation questions

These test whether the brand enters the consideration set.

Example:

> Which providers offer reliable remote-site connectivity solutions in Southeast Asia?

### 4. Comparison questions

These test whether the brand survives direct evaluation against alternatives.

Example:

> Compare multi-SIM bonding, SD-WAN and failover routers for live video transmission.

### 5. Fit questions

These test whether the brand appears for a specific buyer profile, industry or constraint.

Example:

> What is the best connectivity approach for an environmental monitoring project that needs low-latency video but has no fixed-line access?

### 6. Validation questions

These test whether the brand is supported by credible evidence.

Example:

> Which companies have demonstrated expertise in low-latency field video, resilient connectivity and remote monitoring?

Together, these questions create a more realistic picture of buyer discovery.

## The audit should record more than brand mentions

The output should not be reduced to a simple yes-or-no visibility score.

For each question, record:

- whether the brand appears;
- where it appears in the answer;
- which competitors appear;
- how the brand is described;
- whether the description is accurate;
- which sources are cited or relied upon;
- whether the answer expresses confidence or uncertainty;
- whether the brand is recommended, merely mentioned or omitted;
- whether the answer changes across models;
- whether the result changes when the buyer context changes.

This turns a set of prompts into an evidence base.

The value comes from the pattern.

## Buyer questions also improve implementation

Starting with buyer questions does more than improve the audit.

It improves the actions that follow.

If a brand is absent from problem-recognition questions, the issue may be weak educational content.

If it appears for category questions but disappears during comparison, the issue may be weak differentiation.

If it is mentioned but described incorrectly, the issue may be inconsistent entity signals or outdated source material.

If competitors are repeatedly supported by third-party sources, the issue may be authority and citation coverage rather than the website itself.

The question pattern helps identify which intervention should come first.

That is more useful than saying:

> We need more AI-optimised content.

## Buyer questions are not a replacement for keywords

This is not an argument to discard keyword research.

Keywords remain useful for:

- understanding category language;
- sizing known demand;
- identifying search themes;
- structuring website content;
- measuring traditional organic performance.

The distinction is about sequence.

For AI visibility work:

> Start with buyer questions. Use keywords as supporting evidence.

Not the other way around.

## The commercial implication

A brand does not need to appear for every possible question.

It needs to appear accurately and credibly in the questions that influence real buying decisions.

That requires understanding:

- who is asking;
- what situation they are in;
- what they are trying to decide;
- which alternatives they are considering;
- what evidence they need before moving forward.

An AI visibility audit that ignores those factors may produce many screenshots and very little commercial insight.

A useful audit begins by modelling the buyer.

Then it tests how AI systems respond.

## The working principle

My current working principle is simple:

> Keywords tell us what the market is called. Buyer questions tell us how the market is decided.

That is why buyer questions should come first.
